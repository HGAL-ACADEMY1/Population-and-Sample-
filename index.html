Let's start right at the beginning with some clear definitions.

What is a Population? 🤔

In research, when we say Population, we are referring to the total membership of a group, a universe, or objects in a specific state or geographical location. It is the big picture.

Think of it as the totality of the universe from which you could select subjects for a study. Perhaps most importantly, the population is the group to whom the results of your study would be generalized. 🌐

Okay, let’s now move on to the second part of our duo...

What is a Sample? 🧪

A Sample consists of the subjects that you actually want to use in your study. It refers to that smaller section of the population which is selected and from whom data is actually collected.

Imagine you are cooking a huge pot of soup. The whole pot is the population. The spoonful you taste to check for salt? That is your sample. 🍲🥄

Now that we understand this, let’s talk about the specific differences between them.

Differences between Population and Sample ⚖️

While the population is about the generality of the universe or groups of people, the sample refers to merely a fraction of persons from that population.

It is from the sample that data is collected in the course of a study. And here is the key part: because that sample is representative of the population, the results can be generalized back to the population in a scientific study. 🔄

Let’s now dive into how we actually pick these samples. 🧐

Types of Sampling

The types of sampling—often called sampling procedures or methods—are broadly grouped into two main categories:

  • 1️⃣ Scientific or Probability Sampling
  • 2️⃣ Non-Scientific or Non-Probability Sampling

Next, we’ll look at the first category in detail.

1. Scientific Sampling (Probability Sampling) 🎲

This is any sampling method where every member of the population has a greater chance—and in some cases, an equal chance—of being selected as a sample.

Let's break down the different methods within this category:

🔹 Simple Random Sampling

In this method, every member or element of the population has an equal chance of being selected. It is the gold standard of fairness.

The steps involve identifying each member of the population—for example, by listing them with a name or a number—and then selecting the sample through methods like a table of random numbers, the ballot method, or even the throw of a dice. 🎲

🔹 Systematic Random Sampling

Here, the researcher starts randomly. Having listed the population, they select the 'nth' member until the sample size required is reached.

How do we get the 'nth' number? A way of getting the interval is often by dividing the total population by the sample size required. ➗

🔹 Stratified Random Sampling

For this method, the researcher starts by subdividing the population into homogenous subgroups or strata (stratum is the singular). These groups share some known unifying qualities.

Then, the researcher draws a sample from each group using simple random or systematic sampling methods. 🍰

🔹 Cluster or Area Sampling

With cluster sampling, instead of drawing individual members, we select groups or clusters. The population is divided into clusters along geographical boundaries, and specific clusters are randomly selected.

Crucially, all the members of the selected clusters are studied. 🏙️

🔹 Table of Random Numbers

The researcher lists and numbers the members of the population serially. They then select the sample by picking numbers from a standard table of random numbers and including the members corresponding to those numbers. 🔢

At this point, it’s important to consider the other side of the coin.

2. Non-Scientific Sampling (Non-Probability Sampling) 🚫🎲

This is the method of sampling where every member of the population is not given a chance to be part of the sample. The choice usually depends entirely on the judgment of the researcher.

Here are the major types:

🔸 Convenience (Accidental) Sampling

The researcher primarily works with a group that is accessible and convenient. Because of this, the method is prone to bias. ⚠️ There is no evidence that the sample is truly representative of the population of interest.

🔸 Purposive (Judgmental) Sampling

This is where the researcher uses personal decisions to determine the members of the population that suit the specific purposes or requirements of the study. It is subjective. 🧠

🔸 Quota (Proportional) Sampling

Here, the researcher tries to match the proportion of subgroups in the sample as closely as possible to show that every group is represented, but without the randomness of scientific sampling. 📊

Okay, let's wrap this up by comparing the two major approaches.

Differences between Scientific and Non-Scientific Sampling 🥊

The major difference lies in the representativeness and validity of results.

Scientific sampling methods offer every member of the population a chance to be part of the selected sample. In contrast, the non-probability method does not provide this for all members.

Because of this, the non-scientific method is prone to contaminating variables, and the validity of research using them is often questioned. 📉
